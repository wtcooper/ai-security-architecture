# Pattern scan, 2026-11-25

Window: 2026-05-25 to 2026-11-25. No earlier pattern scan is on record, so the window is the last
six months. Compared against 20 patterns. **Narrow scope:** this is a deep dive on one candidate,
"AI app builders that build an application and host it", raised by the November incident sweep
(`docs/research/incidents/2026-11-25.md`, "Fits no pattern"). It is not a full sweep of every lane.
Evidence from 2025 is kept where the catalogue simply missed it, and is marked as older.

## Summary

**Draw one new pattern: the hosted AI app builder.** Someone describes an app to a vendor's AI builder.
The vendor's agent writes the app, gives it a database, sign-in and secrets, and hosts it at a link on
the vendor's domain for other people to use. Each part runs in the vendor's tenancy. The organisation
holds workspace settings and a contract, and the maker holds a publish button.

This is not a product of the agentic software factory. The factory already says so: "Against
prompt-to-app products such as Lovable, Vercel v0, Replit and Bolt … That is a subscribed pattern,
not this one." That subscribed pattern was never drawn. The finder still sends readers who search for
"Lovable", "v0" or "Replit" to the factory, the one drawing that says it is not for them.

The kind is real. At least five independent products ship it generally available (Lovable, Replit,
Bolt, Base44, Vercel v0). Four major vendors launched or extended it in 2026: OpenAI (ChatGPT Sites),
xAI/SpaceXSI (Grok Build), Google (AI Studio Build, which gained a Firebase database and sign-in in
March) and Microsoft (Apps in Copilot Studio and Copilot Cowork, in preview). Incidents landed on every crossing the new drawing would have. The strongest are
Grok Build (10 November 2026), Lovable (2 November 2026), Lovable's own platform regression (February to
April 2026) and Base44's platform-wide sign-in bypass (July 2025).

**The OpenAI question.** The announcement the owner remembers is almost certainly OpenAI DevDay on
6 October 2025, about 14 months ago. OpenAI launched two things that day:

- **Apps in ChatGPT and the Apps SDK.** These are MCP servers that the developer hosts and ChatGPT
  renders. They live on as "plugins". OpenAI publishes no usage figures.
- **AgentKit.** It included a hosted visual Agent Builder. OpenAI announced its wind-down on 3 June 2026,
  and it stops working on 30 November 2026.

Neither of these built and hosted an app. The OpenAI product that does is **ChatGPT Sites**: Codex
builds the site and OpenAI hosts it on `chatgpt.site`. It was announced on 2 June 2026 and is now
available on paid plans. Sites is a product of the new pattern. The Apps SDK is a product of the remote
MCP server pattern.

## New patterns recommended

### Hosted AI app builder (an app the vendor's AI builds and hosts)

- **What it is:** A vendor platform where someone, usually not a developer, describes an app in a chat.
  The vendor's agent writes the code, provisions a database, app sign-in, storage and secrets, writes
  the access rules, and on "Publish" serves the app at a per-app address on the vendor's domain. The
  app gets users of its own, who may be colleagues, invited outsiders or anyone on the internet. The
  organisation's controls are workspace settings, connector approvals and the vendor's assurance. The
  gate between "the agent wrote it" and "it runs on real data" is the maker's publish click, plus
  whatever the workspace admin has made the platform enforce.
- **Use it when:** People describe an app to a vendor's AI builder, and the vendor writes it, gives it
  a database and sign-in, and hosts it at a link for other people to use.

- **Nearest pattern:** Agentic software factory (`cloud-software-factory`). How this one differs:
  - **Who runs each part:** In the factory, the organisation's platform team runs the intake, the
    agent and its sandbox, the AI gateway, the repository, the pipeline, previews, the deploy gate,
    the hosting and each app's data, all in its own cloud. Here one vendor runs every one of those in a
    multi-tenant tenancy, often on its own subprocessors (Supabase, Cloudflare, Mongo, Neon, Firebase).
    The organisation holds a workspace and a contract.
  - **Where the agent runs and what it reaches:** In the factory, the agent sits in a one-task
    sandbox whose egress is the gateway, the package mirror and one repository, and it "holds no cloud
    credential that reaches production". Here the vendor's agent writes code and also configures the
    live backend: it creates the database, writes the row-level or Firestore rules, turns on sign-in,
    and moves schema changes to production on publish. Some vendors now hold the agent back from
    production data (Replit's read replica). That is the vendor's choice, not a control the organisation
    sets.
  - **What crosses a boundary, through what:** In the factory, app users pass the organisation's
    identity provider "whatever the agent wrote", the owner approves each release, and data moves only
    through approved connectors. Here there are four crossings the factory does not have:
    - Visitors from the open internet reach the vendor's hosting through an audience setting the maker
      picks at publish. The default is sometimes "Anyone".
    - In the Supabase-style data plane, each visitor's browser talks straight to the vendor-run
      database with a public key, so the row rules the agent wrote are the only authorisation.
    - The app's own hostname is published in DNS, where passive-DNS services and search engines find it.
    - Other customers of the same platform sit one authorisation check away from the project store and
      from the platform's shared sign-in layer.
  - **Where the controls go and who holds them:** The factory's deploy gate becomes a workspace
    setting the vendor enforces: who may publish externally, blocking publish on findings, approval for
    apps that use connectors. Sign-in in front of the app becomes the vendor's audience gate and login
    methods. Per-app tenant isolation becomes vendor assurance and a bug bounty. The organisation keeps
    discovery of shadow apps, SSO into the builder, connector scopes on its own data, and the contract.
- **Against the other neighbours**, using the catalogue's own "not the same as" text:
  - *UI/low-code managed agent runtime* (`saas-low-code-agent-builder`). Its text: "a vendor platform
    where business and technical teams assemble agents … The reference governs who can build, publish
    and invoke them, which credentials they exercise". There the published thing is an *agent*, invoked
    by colleagues or events, and the question is whose connection it runs on. Here the agent is the
    builder, and the published thing is an *application*: code, its own database, its own sign-in and
    its own users, at a public address. The boxes that carry the incidents are not on that drawing: the
    app's database with row rules, the hosting edge with an audience setting, secrets in the client
    bundle, the per-app domain. **Microsoft now sells both from one studio**: Copilot Studio agents stay
    in the low-code pattern, and Apps in Copilot Studio belong to the new one. These are two findings,
    as the skill says.
  - *Coding & desktop session managed agent runtime* (`saas-hosted-agent-sessions`). Its text: the
    agent "reaches your code only through a scoped install on your code host … review before merge is
    the gate". Here there is no repository of the organisation's (code lives in the vendor's project
    store, and export is optional), no reviewer who reads code, and no merge. The output runs in the
    vendor's hosting with an open lifetime, not a bounded session that is torn down.
  - *Coding agents* (`endpoint-coding-agent-third-party`, `endpoint-coding-agent-first-party`). Their
    text: "the person is the gate", on a machine with a working directory. Here nothing runs on the
    organisation's devices, and the agent's output goes live with a click. Grok's own brand shows the
    split: the **Grok Build CLI** (a coding agent, open-sourced 15 July 2026) belongs with the coding
    agents, and **Build mode in Grok** belongs here.
  - *The test applied:* each of the four questions gets a different answer from the factory and from
    every other pattern. The difference is who runs the parts and where the trust boundaries sit, which
    makes this a **new pattern**. It is not a product of the factory: the factory's own drawing puts each
    box in "our cloud". It is not a change to the factory either: its "not the same as" already rules the
    subscribed form out.
- **Sketch for `pattern-author`:**
  - **Zones and boxes (who runs each):**
    - *Users:* Maker (describes the app, picks its audience at publish, cannot judge the code).
      Workspace admin (sets the defaults the platform enforces). App visitors (workspace members,
      invited outsiders or anyone with the link). The generated front end, holding the public database
      key, runs in each visitor's browser.
    - *Vendor (the builder platform, one tenancy for every customer):*
      - Builder chat and agent: the agent loop on the vendor's model key, with uploads read as
        instruction.
      - Project store: source, chat history (which holds what makers paste, including secrets),
        templates and remixes, with its own "project access" setting, separate from who can visit the
        app.
      - Preview: a link per change, sometimes able to bypass the published audience.
      - Publish step: security scan, blocking optional, audience choice, approvals where the admin set
        them.
      - App hosting edge: per-app hostname on the vendor's domain or a custom domain. The platform's
        audience check and platform sign-in sit here ("Sign in with ChatGPT", Replit "Workspace only",
        Lovable "Workspace", Base44 Private and Workspace, Vercel Authentication or Passport).
      - App backend: database with row or security rules, app-user sign-in, file storage, server
        functions, scheduled jobs, the secrets store, and the app's own model calls on a per-app or
        developer key.
      - Vendor connectors to SaaS and business data.
      - Vendor admin console: the workspace settings.
    - *Our cloud:* enterprise systems of record reached through connectors; our identity provider (SSO
      into the builder, and as an allowed login method for apps); our code host, if the project is
      exported or synced.
    - *External:* the model provider behind the vendor; public package registries the build installs
      from; the internet's discovery layer (passive DNS, search indexing, link previews); and other
      tenants of the same platform, including attackers who use it to host phishing.
    - *Governance (ours):* identity (SSO enforcement, allowed app login methods); policy (workspace
      publish defaults); data access (connector approval and scopes); observability (inventory of
      published apps, shadow-app discovery, takedown); supplier assurance (tenant isolation, bug-bounty
      triage, subprocessors, residency).
  - **Main flows:**
    1. *Build:* maker → builder chat (prompt and uploads) → agent → model provider (vendor key). The
       agent writes code to the project store, provisions the database and writes its rules, enables
       sign-in, stores secrets, and shows a preview.
    2. *Publish:* maker → publish step (scan and audience) → hosting edge goes live at a vendor address.
       The only approval is the maker's, unless the admin required one.
    3. *Use:* visitor → hosting edge (audience check, platform sign-in) → front end in the browser. It
       then reaches **the database directly with the public key** (row rules decide), or server
       functions (secrets), then vendor connectors, then our enterprise data.
    4. *Discovery:* app hostname → passive DNS or search → anyone. Nothing has to be guessed.
    5. *Neighbours:* other platform users → project store or the platform's sign-in API (one
       authorisation check). Remixing a public template turns the vendor's domain into phishing
       hosting.
    6. *Change in place:* maker → agent → live app. A chat edit can change schema or data, and on some
       products every deploy updates the live site, with no staging.
  - **Risks it concentrates** (catalogue IDs):
    - `riskSensitiveDataDisclosure` at the hosting edge and the app database: an open audience,
      missing row rules.
    - `riskInsecureModelOutput` at the agent-written sign-in and rules: inverted checks, membership
      added before it is checked.
    - `riskCrossTenantCredentialPropagation` at the project store and the platform sign-in layer: one
      flaw opens every customer's apps.
    - `riskShadowAndUnknownAgents` for apps with no owner, made in personal or free accounts.
    - `riskPromptInjection` at uploads and remixed templates.
    - `riskRogueActions` at agent → production data.
    - `riskAgenticDelegationConfusedDeputy` at app → connectors, where an app reads on its maker's
      connection.
    - `riskInsecureIntegratedComponent` in the build's packages.
  - **Controls that matter, and who holds each:**
    - Default audience "only me" or "workspace", and block public publishing for non-admins (admin
      setting, vendor-enforced; `D3-APA`). Lovable, Replit, ChatGPT Sites and Base44 all offer it, with
      different defaults.
    - Our SSO enforced for the builder and as an app login method, and unmanaged login methods blocked
      (`D3-APA`, `D3-MFA`).
    - Block publish on critical scan findings and on unresolved sensitive-data findings (admin setting;
      `D3-SYSVA`, `D3-ACH`). It is off by default on Lovable outside new Enterprise workspaces.
    - Row-level or security rules on every table, reviewed before real data. The platform checking rule
      *content*, not just "rules enabled" (`cap-data-access-governance`). Palmer showed that Lovable's
      check covered only the second.
    - No secret in client code: server-side secrets only, and the model key behind the platform rather
      than in the bundle (`D3-CH`). Google moved AI Studio's Gemini key to server-side code; apps
      built before 14 May 2026 are upgraded the next time their Gemini features change.
    - Preview kept apart from production: previews private, preview links off or password-protected,
      the agent unable to write production data, rollback for data as well as code (Replit's split; on
      Sites, restore covers code only).
    - Approval to publish apps that use connectors, with connectors acting as the viewer, not the maker.
    - An inventory of published apps, discovery of apps on builder domains through passive DNS and
      CASB, and a takedown path (`cap-shadow-ai-discovery`, `D3-AI`, `cap-agent-kill-switch`).
    - Supplier assurance on tenant isolation and bug-bounty triage, since both Lovable's 2026 regression
      and Base44's 2025 bypass were platform-side.
- **Where it belongs:** AI services you subscribe to (`surfaceSaas`).
- **Confidence:** High. Five GA products from independent vendors, four major-vendor entries in 2026,
  the catalogue's own text naming the gap, and real incidents on every crossing. One open question for
  the author is the builder that deploys into the customer's own cloud (see Watch), which may need its
  own drawing.

It has the audience and store of the new pattern without server code or an app of its own. Claroc notes these keep their secret in the URL path, not DNS | Server code, its own sign-in, or a database of its own: then it is a product of the new pattern | code.claude.com/docs/en/artifacts.md (undated); support.claude.com/en/articles/9547008-share-artifacts (updated Nov 2026) |
